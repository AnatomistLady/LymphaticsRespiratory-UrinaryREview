Part IV: Applied Physiology & Clinical Answers (86–100)

  1. Lymphedema: The lymphatic system is responsible for returning approximately 2–4 L/day of filtered fluid and leaked plasma proteins from the interstitial space back to the blood. Surgical excision of axillary lymph nodes and severed collecting ducts permanently disrupts drainage channels, causing high-protein fluid to pool in the interstitial space of the ipsilateral arm.
  2. Splenic Rupture: The spleen receives a very high volume of blood directly from the celiac trunk via the large splenic artery. Because the splenic parenchyma consists of delicate, highly vascular red pulp sinusoids enclosed by a thin, fragile capsule, lacerations cause uncontrolled profuse intraperitoneal bleeding, rapidly depleting blood volume and inducing hypovolemic shock.
  3. DiGeorge Syndrome: T lymphocyte precursors travel from the red bone marrow to the thymus to undergo immunocompetence and receptor rearrangement. Without a thymus, immature thymocytes cannot develop into functional helper (CD4+) or cytotoxic (CD8+) T cells, eliminating cell-mediated immunity entirely.
  4. Splenomegaly in Hemolytic Anemia: The red pulp macrophages must phagocytose and break down massive quantities of damaged and fragmented erythrocytes. The high metabolic workload, macrophage proliferation, and congestion of venous sinusoids lead to organ hypertrophy.
  5. Lymphadenopathy: Foreign antigens in the pharynx enter afferent lymphatic vessels and reach cervical nodes. Clonal selection occurs: antigen-specific B cells proliferate in germinal centers, T cells expand in the paracortex, and recruited phagocytes infiltrate the sinuses, causing the node to swell and stretch its capsule.
  6. Capillary Minivalves: When interstitial hydrostatic pressure exceeds lymph capillary internal pressure, the overlapping endothelial edges are pushed open, allowing fluid entry. When interstitial pressure drops or internal lymph pressure rises, the overlapping edges are pressed flat against one another, closing the lumen and preventing backflow.
  7. Anaphylactic Shock: Systemic histamine causes widespread arteriolar vasodilation and extreme venular permeability. Fluid shifts rapidly from vascular compartments into the interstitium, causing a catastrophic drop in total peripheral resistance, dramatic drop in venous return, and severe arterial hypotension.
  8. Passive Immunity: Antivenom supplies exogenous antibodies that neutralize circulating toxin molecules immediately. However, because the recipient’s own B and T cells were not activated and did not undergo clonal expansion, no memory B or T cells are produced; protection vanishes once the antibodies degrade.
  9. HIV and CD4+ Cells: Helper T cells (CD4+) are the master coordinators of adaptive immunity. They secrete cytokines required to fully activate cytotoxic T cells (cell-mediated branch) and activate B cells to undergo class-switching and high-affinity antibody secretion (humoral branch). Losing CD4+ cells collapses both branches.
  10. Splenic Filtration of Encapsulated Bacteria: The spleen's open circulation allows splenic macrophages and resident B cells in the marginal zone/white pulp to recognize, opsonize, and phagocytose polysaccharide-encapsulated bacteria that avoid ordinary phagocytosis in general circulation. Asplenic patients lack this specialized filtration bed and have impaired IgM responses.
  11. Sentinel Node Infiltration: Tumor cells detach from the primary tumor bed, penetrate the permeable endothelial minivalves of initial lymphatic capillaries, travel through collecting lymphatic vessels and afferent lymphatic vessels, and penetrate through the capsule into the subcapsular sinus of the first draining (sentinel) lymph node.
  12. Hypoalbuminemia: Low plasma albumin reduces capillary colloid osmotic pressure ($\pi_c$), which diminishes the inward reabsorption force at venous ends of capillary beds. As a result, net filtration significantly outpaces maximum lymphatic return capacity, producing widespread generalized interstitial edema (ascites/anasarca).
  13. Thymic Selection: Positive selection occurs in the thymic cortex, where thymocytes must recognize host MHC molecules with moderate affinity (promotes self-MHC restriction; non-binding cells die via apoptosis). Negative selection occurs in the medulla/corticomedullary junction, where thymocytes binding self-antigens with high affinity are deleted to prevent autoimmunity (establishing self-tolerance).
  14. Secondary Immune Response: Primary exposure establishes long-lived memory B cells and memory T cells. Upon re-exposure, these memory clones do not require prolonged initial activation; they immediately proliferate and differentiate into IgG-secreting plasma cells, producing an exponential rise in antibody titer within hours to days with higher antigen-binding affinity.
  15. Filariasis (Elephantiasis): Adult parasitic worms physically obstruct inguinal and pelvic collecting lymphatic vessels and induce intense chronic inflammatory fibrosis. Lymphatic return from the lower limbs is blocked, producing chronic pooling of protein-rich lymph, severe fibroblast activation, excess collagen deposition, and massive subcutaneous tissue hypertrophy.
